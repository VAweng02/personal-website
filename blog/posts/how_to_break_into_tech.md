The job market right now is **brutal**. A few years ago, 30–50 applications could land you 10 interviews. Today, some students send 500+ and hear nothing back.

Fewer interviews put a premium on execution. More interviews used to mean more chances. Now, with limited shots, your margin for error is razor-thin.

That’s why execution matters more than ever.

So how do you navigate this? By shifting your priorities.

This blog breaks it down into three parts:
1. **Get an interview** (the hardest step).
2. **Practice**
3. **Execute**

Note: I work in data science, but these lessons apply broadly across tech and adjacent fields. I can’t speak for every industry — only to what I’ve seen firsthand.

## Get an interview
This is the hardest step. There’s no magic formula that guarantees you an interview. Most of what I’ll share you’ve probably heard before — some maybe not — but I’ll keep it concise and move on.

1. **Optimize your resume** — keep it concise, use a standard format, and quantify your impact.
2. **Tap your network** — start with family, friends, and classmates to uncover opportunities.
3. **Attend career fairs** — get face time with recruiters and build connections.
4. **Leverage referrals** — always apply with a referral whenever possible.

None of this is new, but it matters. Let’s move on.

## Practice
This is the most important part — the area where you should invest most of your time.

You can’t guarantee an interview. Networking, referrals, and career fairs help, but luck and timing always play a role. What you can control is how well you prepare and how you perform when the opportunity comes.

Focus on:
1. **Master the fundamentals.** Don’t overlook the basics. In data science, this means being solid in data structures and algorithms, machine learning, statistics and probability, experimentation, SQL, and more. These aren’t optional — they’re the foundation everything else is built on.
2. **Research relentlessly.** Dig into Reddit, Glassdoor, Blind, and anywhere else you can find insights. Learn the format, the question types, and the timing. You won’t uncover everything, but you’ll avoid most surprises.
3. **Communicate your work.** Explaining your work is harder than it seems. Practice walking through problems out loud until it feels natural. The smoother your technical execution, the easier your communication becomes.
4. **Balance speed and quality.** A correct solution delivered too late is useless. Practice until accuracy and speed go hand-in-hand.

### Behavioral
Almost every company includes this section, and while it’s often the most straightforward, preparation still matters. A few things that helped me:
1. Know how to introduce yourself (most people don't have a structured way and rambles)
2. Be able to explain your experience and projects thoroughly. It's pretty easy to tell if you don't understand your experiences/projects that well.
3. Prepare a few go-to stories (e.g., leadership, conflict, high impact) that you can mold to different questions. This saves you from scrambling on the spot.

## Execute
You’ve gotten in the door and done the prep — now it’s time to execute. Opportunities like this are rare, which is why preparation matters so much: it gives you the confidence and clarity to perform at your best.

Keep these in mind:
1. **Ask questions.** Don’t jump in blindly. Many prompts are intentionally open-ended — the interviewer expects you to clarify. Asking the right questions puts you on the path to the right solution.
2. **Show your thinking.**  Even if you’re stuck, narrate your approach. Break the problem down, state assumptions, and outline possible paths. Interviewers reward structured problem-solving and resilience. However, they don't reward silence.
 3. **Don’t let mistakes derail you.** Perfection isn’t expected (unless it’s a major error). What matters more is how you respond. The real danger isn’t the mistake itself — it’s letting it throw you off for the rest of the interview. Acknowledge it, adjust, and move forward.
4. **Close strong.** If time allows, always ask the interviewer questions about their role, team, or company. It signals genuine interest and leaves a lasting impression.

## Will This Guarantee You a Job?

No — nothing can. But this mindset took me from zero interview offers early in undergrad to multiple offers in my last two recruiting cycles, despite the fact that I landed fewer interviews later on. Why? Because I maximized the opportunities I did get.

Focus less on what you _can’t_ control, and double down on execution when you get your chance.
